Four-camera basketball court, NVIDIA DeepStream 9.1 Multi-View 3D Tracking (MV3DT), evaluated on a Dell XPS 8960 (RTX 3060 Ti 8 GB) with deployment on a Jetson Orin Nano Super in mind. Date: 2026-10-25.

All results come from DeepStream runs on the final calibration, which places every camera on its physical mount (section 5): three runs (mount_r1-mount_r3) for the main results, three more with the court tracker running inside DeepStream (ct3_r1-ct3_r3, section 4.8). All court-tracker figures were regenerated with the final code (scripts/phase8_reevaluate.sh).

1. Summary

Recommendation. Use DeepStream for detection, per-camera tracking and jersey reading. Then run the court tracker developed here on its output and report each player by identity (team + jersey number), not by track ID.

What is reported Identity IDF1 (3 DeepStream runs x 2 labelled minutes)
MV3DT global IDs as delivered 52.4 % (38-61 %)
Court tracker, track IDs 68.3 % (58-79 %)
Court tracker, player identities (recommended) 79.2 % (74-89 %)
Court tracker, player identities, every input causal (deployable form, section 4.6) 80.4 % (72-90 %)
The same, running inside DeepStream in C++ (3 further runs, section 4.8) 80.0 % (71-87 %)

IDF1 here is the share of labelled detections that end up on the output unit matched to the right player. It's measured on hand-labelled ground truth (section 2.3).

  • Speed on the 3060 Ti: the DeepStream pipeline with jersey reading runs at about 56 fps per camera (real time is 30). The court tracker takes about 1.9 ms per frame on one CPU core in Python, and about 0.1 ms in its C++ port, which gives identical output (section 4.7).

  • Running live inside DeepStream (section 4.8): the C++ court tracker and the automatic team model run as part of the pipeline and send one Kafka message per frame with every player's identity and court position. The pipeline still runs at about 58 fps per camera. The live output is reproduced exactly by both the C++ and the Python versions replaying its recorded inputs, and scores the same as the Python version on the same runs.

  • Seated spectators are dropped (section 4.9). Front-row spectators used to reach the output and take players' identities. A geometric rule now removes them (from 61 % of their detections in the output to 4 %, with no identities), at no cost to the player score.

  • Still not good enough for reliable per-player statistics. About 20 % of player time is credited to the wrong player or no player. Most of that is a wrong identity (about 15 %), not a missing one (about 5 %; section 4.4).

  • Two inputs were prepared offline. The court tracker's decisions are causal, but the results in sections 4 and 6 use a per-camera position correction and a team colour model, both derived from the whole recording; the colour clusters were also named by hand. Replacing them with what a live system has (a correction measured once at installation, and team colours found automatically as the game runs) scores 80.4 %. That is at least as good as the offline version; the difference is within run-to-run noise (section 4.6).

  • Results vary from run to run. Repeated DeepStream runs of the same configuration give different MV3DT IDs and jersey reads. Per-minute identity IDF1 ranges from 74 % to 89 %, so every number here is an average over three runs.

  • Measured deployment options (section 6):

    • INT8 detector: 55 % faster than FP16 on the 3060 Ti, at 4-7 points lower end-to-end identity accuracy.

    • Detector every 2nd or 3rd frame: costs 18-29 points with MV3DT and barely speeds anything up, so it's ruled out.

    • Jetson (estimated from these measurements): four cameras at 30 fps look borderline to feasible on an AGX Orin with INT8, and not feasible on an Orin Nano Super.

  • Camera placement matters more than the number of cameras. Moving the same four cameras from the backboards to high corner mounts would raise the share of the court seen by two cameras at jersey-readable size, from clearly different directions, from 20 % to about 80 % (simulated). Six high wall cameras would reach about 96 %, but probably need more compute than the Orin Nano Super offers (section 7).

2. Setup

2.1 Hardware and software

  • Ubuntu 24.04, RTX 3060 Ti 8 GB, driver 580, Docker with the NVIDIA Container Toolkit.

  • DeepStream 9.1: container nvcr.io/nvidia/deepstream:9.1-triton-multiarch, repo NVIDIA/DeepStream tag v9.1.0.2, app deepstream-test5-app with a small patch (court filter, torso objects, jersey log, court tracker).

  • Brokers: mosquitto (MQTT, inter-camera tracker messages) and Kafka 4.2.0 (MV3DT metadata).

2.2 Cameras and video

  • Mounting: two camera pairs, each on one apparatus on top of a backboard, 6-10 in apart. NL + FR sit at the left end, NR + FL at the right end. The N cameras look steeply down at the near half; the F cameras look across at the far half.

  • Video: the cameras record 4K; this pipeline uses a 1920 × 1080, 30 fps, undistorted transcode of about 15 minutes. 0:00-7:00 is a game (red vs maroon, 2 referees); after that comes a warm-up, which is out of scope.

  • Calibration: one 3 × 4 projection matrix per camera, in metres with the origin at centre court, fitted with the cameras on their mounts (section 5).

2.3 Ground truth

Two labelled minutes of the game: 2:00-3:00 and 4:00-5:00. Each has 30 moments, 2 s apart. Every person on court was labelled at each moment, giving about 380 labelled groups per minute. About 325 and 343 detections per minute carry a full identity, across about 10 people per minute (players from both teams, including substitutes).

  • Labelling: done by the user on a web page, with the tracker's guess shown but not counted.

  • Scoring: labels attach to detection keys (frame, camera, box), so any tracker that uses the same detections can be scored. They carry over to new DeepStream runs by box overlap.

  • Refs: both referees were labelled only as "ref", so referees aren't scored.

  • Limit: both minutes have been used for development decisions, so there is no untouched held-out data left (section 8).

3. Pipeline

  1. Detection: PeopleNet 2.6.3 (FP16, 960 × 544, every frame, batch of 4 cameras). PeopleNet Transformer and RT-DETR were also tried; they ran far slower (about 10 and 21 fps per camera) with no tracking advantage.

  2. Court filter (custom probe): drops detections whose floor point lies outside the court plus 0.75 m (benches, crowd, scorer's table).

  3. MV3DT (NvDCF tracker with the single-view 3D person model, multi-view association over MQTT). Provides per-camera tracklets and 3D foot positions.

  4. Jersey reading inside DeepStream:

    • torso objects are taken from player boxes at least 150 px tall
    • a text detector (PP-OCRv4 det, 384 × 480) finds text boxes
    • a digit recogniser (PP-OCRv4 rec, 48 × 64) reads 1-2 digit numbers

    About 14.5 reads per second over the game; 94.5-94.9 % of reads checked against the ground truth are correct.

  5. Court tracker (tools/world_tracker.py, Python, causal, runs on the Kafka output). A court-plane Kalman tracker:

    • matching: direction-aware measurement noise and a likelihood cost
    • identity cues: team colour (torso histograms) and jersey numbers, where identity = team + number
    • identity output: an online roster and a per-player appearance model

    • identity freshness: identities come from recent reads (10 s), and a track splits when a majority of its recent reads show another number

    • seated spectators: detections whose box bottom and MV3DT foot point disagree in the way seated people do are dropped before tracking (section 4.9)

    The same tracker runs inside DeepStream as a C++ probe (apps/courttrack, section 4.8).

    All tracking decisions are causal; the output is final as soon as it's made. Three of its inputs and filters were, however, prepared offline from the whole recording for every result in sections 4 and 6:

    • camera position bias: a constant offset per camera (mainly NL −0.18 m and NR +0.18 m along the court), estimated from detections of the same person in two cameras over the whole run and subtracted before tracking

    • team colour model: torso colours clustered over the game's frames, with the clusters named by hand (red, maroon, referee, other)

    • spectator filter: tracks that never moved and sat near a line are removed after the run

    Section 4.6 measures causal replacements for all three.

4.3 Run-to-run variation

  • Detections are almost the same: two DeepStream runs of the same configuration share 98.7 % of their boxes.

  • Everything built on them differs: the MV3DT IDs, per-camera tracklets and the jersey reads attached to them come out differently, most likely because the order in which cameras exchange tracker messages varies.

  • Effect on scores: per-minute identity IDF1 of the default ranges from 72 % to 84 % across the three runs. All comparisons here are therefore paired by run and minute and averaged over three runs. Differences under about 2 points aren't reliable.

4.4 Where accuracy is still lost

  • Wrong number carried over from another player (8.6 %): a track swaps players and keeps the old identity until enough new reads arrive. Reads are sparse: a given player is read only every several seconds.

  • Wrong number, other (4.9 %): misreads, and identities established from too few reads.

  • Unidentified (4.5 %): almost all of it (4.3 %) on tracks with readable crops and reads that never reached an identity.

  • Wrong team with the right number (1.6 %): on the numbers both teams wear (1 and 3).

  • Missed by the tracker (0.7 %): small; part of it is the seated-spectator rule (section 4.9). Detection and court filtering aren't the bottleneck.

  • Camera bias: very stable across runs (NL −0.17 to −0.20 m, NR +0.17 to +0.19 m), so a value measured at installation and stored with the calibration works (tools/calib_bias.py writes it into each camera's file as courtTrackerBias_m). It must be re-measured whenever the calibration changes. Without any correction the score drops 2.8 points (5 of 6 pairs worse); an estimate from the first 2 minutes did about as well as the whole-run one here.

  • Automatic team colours (tools/online_teams.py), with no operator:

    • each court track keeps a colour summary of its torso crops; every 5 s, the summaries of the last 3 minutes are clustered

    • the two clusters whose tracks get the most jersey reads per detection are the teams (players' numbers are read, referees' and spectators' hardly ever); everything else, referees included, gets no team label

    • team names A/B follow their cluster from one clustering to the next, so identities (A:23) stay stable

    • per-camera colour correction is learned as the game runs; the first model forms after 30 s, and before it no detection has a team label

    • each re-clustering starts from the previous centres, which keeps the result nearly independent of the random start of the first model

  • Four clusters (a separate referee cluster) do much worse, −9.4 points; the reason hasn't been investigated.

  • Checked crop by crop against the hand-named model, all three runs (3 clusters, seed 0):

    • Teams: maroon was always A. Red was B, except in the first minute: between 0:30 (first model) and 1:00, 26-37 % of red crops were labelled A, and between 1:00 and 2:00 only 1-14 crops per run. From 2:00 to the end of the game, no crop got the other team's name.

    • Referees: one referee crop per run got a team label.

    • Unchecked: team A also labels about 6,000-6,400 crops per run that the hand-named model left unlabelled. It hasn't been checked whether those are players or people beside the court.

  • Caution:

    • Selection bias: the online model's settings (3 clusters, the read-rate rule) were chosen on these same runs and minutes, and the gains are within the run-to-run noise. So the fair reading is "at least as good as the offline version", not better.

    • One game: it's tested on one game only.

    • The read-rate rule assumes players' numbers are read clearly more often than anyone else's shirt. Here team clusters got reads on 2-4 % of detections, the rest on 0.1-0.3 %.

  • Deployment uses the fixed bias and the online 3-cluster model, without the spectator filter.

4.7 C++ port of the court tracker

apps/courttrack is a C++ port of the court tracker's default configuration (courttrack.cpp) and of the online team model (online_teams.cpp), for use inside the DeepStream pipeline. Both were checked by feeding them the exact per-frame inputs of the Python version (recorded with tools/courttrack_export.py, replayed with apps/courttrack/ct_replay):

  • Same output: on mount_r1-r3, all of about 516,000 detection identities per run are identical to the Python version's, in both the offline configuration and the deployable one (section 4.6), as are identity IDF1 and switch counts on both labelled minutes.

  • Speed: 0.08-0.11 ms per frame on one core of the i7, against 1.9 ms for the Python version. The team model's re-clustering every 5 s takes up to about 6 ms.

4.8 Running inside DeepStream

The court tracker now runs live in the pipeline (scripts/run_mv3dt.sh with COURTTRACK=1; probe apps/courttrack/courttrack_probe.cpp, after the jersey reader):

  • Inputs: MV3DT's world foot points, the court filter (0.3 m margin), the fixed bias from the camera files, the jersey reads of the same frame, and torso colour histograms. Every 3rd frame, the torso of each good crop (FL, FR, NR) is cut out of the frame with NVIDIA's NvBufSurfTransform into a 32 × 48 tile and its hue x saturation histogram computed on the CPU. This works the same way on a Jetson.

  • Processing: once all four cameras of a frame have arrived, the online team model labels the crops, the court tracker steps, and the team model learns from the tracker's assignment.

  • Output: one JSON message per frame on its own Kafka topic (courttrack) with, per player: court track id, identity (e.g. A:23) and team, position and velocity on the court, whether it was seen this frame or predicted, how many cameras saw it, and the detections it came from (camera + MV3DT object id). The per-camera messages are unchanged. Consumers should key on the track id: a track's identity can change (when first established, or after a swap is detected), and a briefly unseen player is kept with a predicted position for up to 2 s.

  • Logs for evaluation: the identity of every detection (CSV) and the tracker's inputs, which ct_replay and the Python reference can replay.

Checked on three new full-game runs (ct3_r1-ct3_r3, FP16, no display, with the seated-spectator rule):

  • Live vs Python reference: the same score (80.0 % against 79.1 %; 2 run-minutes better, 4 within half a point). The live pipeline computes the torso histograms differently (NV12 frames scaled to small tiles, against OpenCV on full-resolution frames). In three earlier live runs, replaying the live inputs with the OpenCV histograms swapped in changed the score by 0.5 points with no consistent direction: the two kinds of histogram differ noticeably, but the team model learns from whichever it gets.

  • Exact reproduction needed three fixes, which apply to the Python reference too:

    • Duplicate ids: MV3DT occasionally gives two different people the same object id in one camera and frame (70-200 times per game, several metres apart). Everything downstream is keyed by (frame, camera, id), so now only the first is kept. Because tracking decisions amplify small input changes, this alone moved the main default from 78.3 % to 77.8 % (the seated-spectator rule then moved it to 79.2 %); all court-tracker figures in this report were regenerated with both.

    • Track identity: the roster counted tracks by memory address, which a new track can reuse after a merge; tracks now carry a permanent serial number.

    • Arithmetic: the colour statistics are summed in a fixed order (not NumPy's pairwise or BLAS summation), fused multiply-add is disabled in the C++ builds (-ffp-contract=off, which matters on the Jetson's ARM CPU), and NumPy's percentile formula is matched exactly.

  • Speed in the pipeline: the same computation takes about 0.1 ms per frame when replayed alone, against 1.3 ms in the pipeline, presumably because it shares the CPU with DeepStream's threads (not investigated). The slowest frames are the team model's re-clustering, which runs on the probe's streaming thread. On a Jetson, this is the part to watch: it can be moved to a worker thread with its result applied a fixed number of frames later, which keeps the output reproducible.

  • Logging: writing the evaluation logs to this PC's hard disk stalls for up to 3.6 s at times. The logs are for evaluation only and would be off in deployment. The Kafka send is timed together with them (2.4-2.6 ms per frame on average, including the stalls).

4.9 Seated spectators

The labels include 160 crops of people who aren't players (about 450 detections per set of three runs). Almost all of them are spectators in the front-row seats, seen by FR (414 of the 453 in the mount runs), which looks across the court at them (tools/nonplayer_eval.py, reports/nonplayer_output*.md).

  • Why they got through: their boxes end at the line, so the court filter keeps them. MV3DT's body model assumes a standing person, so it places their feet about 2 m inside the court (median), among the players.

  • What it cost: they took real players' identities (A:1, B:0, A:3, ...), so the output carried the same identity twice, and players passing by got merged into their tracks. The identity score didn't show it, because it is scored on players only; the spectator filter of section 4.6 didn't catch them either, since these tracks aren't static.

  • The rule: for a seated person, the box bottom projected onto the floor lands at their seat, at or just outside the line, while MV3DT puts the feet 2 m further in. For players the two agree to about 0.3 m. A detection is now dropped before tracking when its two foot points are more than 1 m apart and its box-bottom point is less than 0.6 m inside the lines (boxes cut by the bottom image edge are never dropped). On the labelled detections this catches 96 % of the spectators and 0.5 % of the players. Body height alone separates them poorly (23 % of spectators under 1.4 m, against 3 % of players). No pose model is needed; both points are already available, in Python and in the DeepStream probe.

5. Calibration

The final calibration places each camera where it physically is: the pairs on their backboard apparatus.

  • Line tracing: each camera's court lines were re-traced automatically from a player-free median image (tools/court_refine.py). This replaced hand-traced lines; on FL those had followed the "LET'S HOOP" lettering and the logo instead of the paint, and its overlay was visibly rotated (spotted by the user).

  • Joint fit on the mounts (tools/calib_mount.py): all four cameras are fitted together, with each far camera held within 0.25 m per axis of its partner (FL with NR, FR with NL) and the standard HS/NCAA markings. The court's length and width are fitted.

  • Court size: fitted at 70.3 × 45.1 ft. All tools read it from tools/court.py.

  • Single source of truth: the camera files are in datasets/gym/camInfo. Earlier versions are archived beside them, and each run keeps the copy it used in camInfo_used/.

  • Independent check: the 3-point arc (19.75 ft) wasn't used in the fit, yet it lands on the painted arc (FL shown below).

Position consistency between cameras. For the same labelled person at the same moment, MV3DT foot points from two cameras differ by a median 0.37 m (18 % of pairs over 0.9 m; 1,114 pairs over three runs). The error runs mostly along each camera's line of sight. It comes from estimating feet out of boxes (posture, boxes cut by the frame edge), not from the calibration.

6. Deployment options: measured on the 3060 Ti, estimated for Jetson

6.1 Measured: INT8, detector interval and no display

Before any of these is proposed for a Jetson, each was run on the 3060 Ti (three full DeepStream runs per variant unless noted, all without on-screen display or annotated video) and scored on the ground truth. End-to-end counts correctly identified labelled detections over all labelled detections, so people a variant fails to detect count as errors.

Full tables: reports/deploy_end_to_end.md, reports/deploy_variants_detection.md, reports/deploy_idf1_*.md.

  • No display: about 8 % faster (61 vs 57 fps per camera) with identical output. Assumed for every embedded configuration below.

  • INT8 detector:

    • Speed: 55 % faster than FP16 (95 vs 61 fps per camera).

    • Accuracy: 4-7 points lower end-to-end (71.2 % vs 74.8-78.3 %). INT8 detects about 4 % fewer labelled people (players 93.4 % vs 98.7 %, non-players 70.8 % vs 94.4 %; reports/deploy_int8_by_category.md).

    • Lower detection threshold: lowering it from 0.2 to 0.1 recovers most of the missed people (97.9 % detected, players 96.9 %), but hardly the accuracy: end-to-end 72.7 % against 71.2 %, because the people it adds are identified less well (identity IDF1 on detected labels 74.2 % vs 75.2 %). So missed detections don't explain INT8's loss. INT8 boxes also sit differently: only 88-89 % of them match an FP16 box (IoU ≥ 0.5), against 98.6 % between two FP16 runs. That suggests box placement, which feeds the floor positions, as the likelier cause. Not adopted.

    • Calibration: NVIDIA's published calibration file was made with an older TensorRT (10.3), which names the activation layers differently. With it, most layers fell back to full precision and the detector was slower than FP16. The runs above use a calibration made with the deployment TensorRT version (10.16) on 400 frames from the video (tools/int8_calibrate.py). A Jetson needs a calibration made with its own TensorRT version.

  • Detector every 2nd or 3rd frame (DeepStream interval): not usable with MV3DT.

    • Accuracy: it costs 18-29 points end-to-end against the three FP16 runs.

    • Speed: it barely helps (every 3rd frame is even slower than every frame). GPU use falls while speed doesn't rise, so the bottleneck moves to MV3DT's own tracking work.

    • Output rate: with MV3DT's visual tracker off, positions come out only on detector frames.

    • Tracker changes needed: MV3DT drops a new track the first time it misses a detection, so the runs needed a longer early-termination window (3 frames for every 2nd frame, 4 for every 3rd), and the court tracker's frame-counting rules had to be scaled to the detection rate.

    NVIDIA's MV3DT configurations always detect on every frame.

    • Its two DLAs could take the INT8 detector off the GPU (DeepStream supports this), but their throughput for this model must be measured.

    • 4K decoding: four 4K streams for the hybrid jersey reading (section 7.3) are within its decoder, not the Orin Nano Super's.

Recommendation.

  • For four cameras at 30 fps (current or corner layout), choose the AGX Orin. The 32 GB version may be enough; the 64 GB version adds CPU cores and memory rather than much GPU.

  • The Orin Nano Super would need fewer cameras or a lower frame rate, and a 15 fps test should come first.

  • Before buying, measure on the device:
    • fps per camera (INT8, no display)
    • DLA throughput (AGX Orin)
    • decode capacity
    • accuracy, re-scored with tools/multi_eval.py after re-calibrating INT8 for the device's TensorRT

7. Camera placement and quantity

The current cameras sit at backboard height and see players mostly from the side. That is the main reason tracks swap in close encounters (section 4.4). This section compares candidate layouts with a camera-layout simulator (tools/camera_layout.py) and sets them against compute.

7.1 Method

  • Model: each camera is a 1920 × 1080 pinhole at a given position, aim point and lens field of view, over the fitted court. For each 0.5 m floor cell and a standing 1.8 m player, it computes:

    • which cameras see the whole player
    • the player's height in pixels: at least 150 px is readable for the jersey reader, at least 80 px detectable

    • how steeply each camera looks down, and the floor length hidden behind a player ("shadow"; shorter means fewer occlusions)

    • the largest angle between two readable cameras' viewing directions. At least 45° apart is needed to triangulate positions and separate players who overlap in one view.

  • Validation: for the current cameras, real detection boxes measure 0.92-1.02 times the predicted standing height (median per camera, mount_r1). So the model's pixel sizes are realistic.

  • Fair comparison: each new camera's lens (50-95° horizontal) and aim point are chosen by a search that maximises the area seen by two readable cameras at least 45° apart. Existing cameras stay as calibrated.

  • Venue assumptions, to be replaced by measurements: walls 2 m beyond the lines, wall mounts up to 6 m high. Room dimensions and ceiling height weren't available.

  • DLAs: on the AGX Orin, running the detector on its DLAs would free GPU time; not measured.

  • Detector interval: not an option with MV3DT (section 6.1).

  • Jersey reading scales with the number of readable players rather than cameras. More cameras can be held to the same read budget.

  • Inter-camera tracker messages (MQTT) grow with the number of camera pairs. Six cameras is modest.

  • The cameras already record 4K; this pipeline uses a 1080p transcode. The detector runs at 960 × 544 either way. So feeding the 4K streams costs mainly decoding (about 4× the decode work, to be checked on the Orin Nano Super), while the jersey reader would get torso crops with twice the pixels.

    • What it wouldn't change: with the current cameras, the simulator shows readable coverage unchanged (94 % of the court by at least one camera). Wherever a camera sees a player at all, the player is already at least 150 px tall at 1080p.

    • What it could change: read reliability (more reads and fewer misreads on sharp crops), which would shorten the wrong-identity periods after swaps (section 4.4).

    • Lighter variant if decode is the limit: a 1080p stream for detection and tracking plus a 4K stream at 5-10 fps for reading only.

7.4 Proposal

  1. Same compute as today (4 streams): move to layout C, four corner cameras at about 5 m.

    • Lenses about 60° horizontal, each aimed diagonally across the court.
    • Two-camera, well-separated readable coverage rises from 20 % to about 84 %.

    • Views become steeper (median 15° → 20°), and the area hidden behind players shrinks from about 9 m to about 6 m.

    • This is the best accuracy per unit of compute. With four streams it needs an AGX Orin at 30 fps (section 6.2).

    • If six streams are affordable, use layout F: four sideline cameras at the quarter lines plus one per baseline, at 5-6 m, for about 96 % coverage. Six streams at 30 fps exceed an AGX Orin's GPU by the estimates in section 6.2, unless its DLAs take the detector or the cameras run at a lower frame rate (both untested).

  2. If cameras can't move: keep the far backboard cameras and move the two near ones to the sideline walls at half court (layout E, still 4 streams, 65 %). Adding two cameras to the current four (layout B, 6 streams, 70 %) costs more compute and still does worse than four corner cameras.

  3. Whatever the layout:

    • use identical cameras with locked exposure and focus
    • synchronise frames (hardware sync or accurate timestamps)
    • avoid pointing at windows or bright lights
    • calibrate with the mount positions as a constraint, as in section 5, ideally with measured court dimensions

Caveat. These are geometric coverage figures, not tracking accuracy. Before buying hardware, the best check is a temporary installation (stands or poles at the corners) for one session, run through the same pipeline and scored on newly labelled ground truth.

How the professional systems compare (general knowledge, not verified here):

  • Basketball: NBA optical tracking (SportVU, Second Spectrum, now Hawk-Eye) uses many fixed cameras high in the arena rafters.

  • American football: the NFL uses RFID chips in the shoulder pads.

  • Identity: in all of these, operators assign identities, correct swaps live and review data after games, with rosters and substitutions from official feeds.

  • What this means here: high mounts, roster and substitution input, and a light operator correction step are the parts worth borrowing. All of them fit this project's real-time setup.

8. Limitations

  • Small ground truth: two minutes of one game on one court, with 10 players and refs not scored.

  • No clean held-out data: both labelled minutes informed development decisions. A third labelled minute would give an unbiased final number.

  • Run-to-run variation: per-minute identity IDF1 ranges from 74 % to 89 % across DeepStream runs. Three runs per configuration narrow it, but differences under about 2 points aren't reliable.

  • Court-specific settings: the main results use a team colour model clustered per game with hand-named clusters. The automatic online model that replaces it (sections 4.6 and 4.8) is tested on this one game only. The roster is inferred online. The seated-spectator rule's thresholds were set on this venue. Other courts, lighting and kits are untested.

  • Sensitivity: tracking decisions amplify tiny input changes. Removing 130-200 duplicate detections per game (out of about 550,000) moved the main result by half a point, and individual run-minutes by up to 3 points. Only differences well above the run-to-run spread should be trusted.

  • Court size and venue: the court's length and width are fitted, not measured. The camera-placement study assumes wall distances and mounting heights.

The roster can't say which colour is which team, but the tracker can learn that from which numbers each colour cluster is read with. This also targets the wrong-team errors on shared numbers.
